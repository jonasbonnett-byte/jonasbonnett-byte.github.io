Case Study 04Cell Battery Research and Development — Electrode Processing Improvements

Author: Jonas Bonnett — Associate of Applied Science, Manufacturing Engineering Technology, Hennepin Technical College (2013)
Fictional setting: VoltCore Battery Labs (fictional company) — Cell Battery Research & Development
Illustrative role: Lead Assembler, Cell Battery Research and Development

FICTIONAL ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: This case study is written to demonstrate electrode-processing improvement method. The company, department, and figures are fictionalized and do not describe work performed at any real employer. It is not a record of employment. All figures below are adjusted representative values based on real-world experience, not exact records.

Context

This example follows a Lead Assembler with years of research and development deployments behind him, grounded in an Associate of Applied Science in Manufacturing Engineering Technology. The craft is taking a process that works once in a scientist’s hands and making it work the same way every time on the floor — with tooling, fixtures, and discipline that hold up under production.

In this fictional setting, the Cell Battery research and development department faced three problems slowing it down: electrodes that went missing in storage, curled anodes that needed straightening without damage, and cathode tab ablation done by hand with a razor blade. The example works all three, and each one ends the same way: a validated baseline with charge and discharge data in the scientists’ hands for review toward implementation.

Challenge

  • Electrode storage was disorganized: electrodes were hard to find and easy to lose through improper placement. Lost electrodes are potential bottlenecks — every minute spent hunting for one, and every electrode lost, stalls the work downstream.
  • Single-sided asymmetric anodes arrived curled and needed de-curling before processing — without applying so much heat or force that the electrode was damaged.
  • Cathode tab ablation was manual: scraping tabs with a razor blade took a long time, risked wrist strain over time, and — without a proper fixture — did not give consistent data output from electrode to electrode.

What was done

  • Reorganized electrode storage for easy access — a process improvement built on a craftsman’s rule: everything has a place, and the place serves the work. Electrodes are separated and organized by department and requester, so each one can be found quickly and returned correctly — cutting the time wasted looking for them and minimizing electrodes lost to improper placement, removing a bottleneck the department had learned to live with.
  • Introduced a hair straightener as a low-heat de-curling instrument for single-sided asymmetric anodes — applying minimal, controlled heat to de-curl the anodes without damaging them. The baseline was built around the method: the de-curled anodes passed through testing and validation, producing charge and discharge data for the scientists to review for implementation in electrode processing.
  • Replaced manual razor-blade tab ablation with a Dremel, held in a purpose-built fixture — determining through testing how much pressure, height, and Dremel power effectively clean the tab without manual scraping and without damaging it. The fixture holds the Dremel at a set height while applying enough pressure to clean the tab consistently, electrode after electrode — no wrist strain, no variation from hand pressure, no dependence on one operator’s touch. The baseline built on this method was approved by the scientists and passed through testing and validation, with charge and discharge data acquired for review in process implementation for the Cell Battery department.

Results

  • Electrode storage: most technicians spent 25–35 minutes a week hunting for electrodes in a bin mixed with other electrodes — and when one could not be found, up to an hour of unproductive time. With the system implemented, that fell to 12–18 minutes a week.
  • Anode de-curling baseline: single-sided asymmetric anode coupons de-curled from a 20mm curl to 10mm with minimal heat via the hair-straightener method — the most effective approach; baseline passed testing and validation; charge and discharge data delivered to scientists for review toward implementation in electrode processing.
  • Cathode tab-ablation baseline: manual razor-blade scraping carried a 12–18% defect rate — damaged tabs and coupons from too much pressure and incorrect blade angles. In a 10-minute head-to-head, manual ablation produced 50 coupons; the Dremel method produced 70, effortlessly — about 40% more in the same time, with fewer defects. Baseline approved by scientists and passed testing and validation; charge and discharge data acquired for review in process implementation for the Cell Battery department. Consistent output by design — the fixture, not the operator’s hand, sets the height and pressure.

Why it matters

Mastery in this field is not one invention — it is the discipline of making good work repeatable. A lost electrode, a curled anode, a hand-scraped tab: each one looks small until you multiply it across a department, and each one is a place where craftsmanship either holds the line or lets it slip. The work here was to see those points clearly, build the tool or the system that holds them, and prove it with data the scientists could trust — so the process no longer depended on who happened to be holding the blade.
