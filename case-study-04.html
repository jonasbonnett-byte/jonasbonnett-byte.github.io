Case Study 04Cell Battery Research and Development — Electrode Processing Improvements

Author: Jonas Bonnett — Associate of Applied Science, Manufacturing Engineering Technology, Hennepin Technical College (2013)
Fictional setting: VoltCore Battery Labs (fictional company) — Cell Battery Research & Development
Illustrative role: Lead Assembler, Cell Battery Research and Development

FICTIONAL ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: This case study is written to demonstrate electrode-processing improvement method. The company, department, and figures are fictionalized and do not describe work performed at any real employer. It is not a record of employment. All figures below are adjusted representative values based on real-world experience, not exact records.

Context

This example follows a Lead Assembler with years of research and development deployments behind him, grounded in an Associate of Applied Science in Manufacturing Engineering Technology. The craft is taking a process that works once in a scientist’s hands and making it work the same way every time on the floor — with tooling, fixtures, and discipline that hold up under production.

In this fictional setting, the Cell Battery research and development department faced a problem slowing it down: electrodes that went missing in storage. The example works it through — a system, a baseline, and the numbers to prove it.

Challenge

  • Electrode storage was disorganized: electrodes were hard to find and easy to lose through improper placement. Lost electrodes are potential bottlenecks — every minute spent hunting for one, and every electrode lost, stalls the work downstream.

What was done

  • Reorganized electrode storage for easy access — a process improvement built on a craftsman’s rule: everything has a place, and the place serves the work. Electrodes are separated and organized by department and requester, so each one can be found quickly and returned correctly — cutting the time wasted looking for them and minimizing electrodes lost to improper placement, removing a bottleneck the department had learned to live with.

Results

  • Electrode storage: most technicians spent 25–35 minutes a week hunting for electrodes in a bin mixed with other electrodes — and when one could not be found, up to an hour of unproductive time. With the system implemented, that fell to 12–18 minutes a week.

Why it matters

Mastery in this field is not one invention — it is the discipline of making good work repeatable. A lost electrode looks small until you multiply it across a department — every minute spent hunting for one stalls the work downstream. The work here was to see that point clearly, build the system that holds it, and prove it with numbers the team could trust — so the process no longer depended on who happened to remember where things were.
