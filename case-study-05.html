Case Study 05UAV Interior Hardware — Retrofitting Brackets for Flight Hardware and Ground Control Systems

Author: Jonas Bonnett — Mechanical CAD Drafter / Designer II, a major aerospace defense contractor (via contract), 04/2016–05/2017, San Diego, CA
Platforms: Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) platforms — plus ground control systems
Tools: PTC Creo Parametric, Windchill, GD&T

Note: Defense-aerospace work, described at résumé level only — no proprietary drawings, part numbers, dimensions, or program details beyond what is already on his public résumé.

Context

Interior mechanical components for unmanned aerial vehicle platforms, plus ground control systems, in initial-release production. Design work in PTC Creo Parametric, with drawing records maintained in Windchill.

The work sat at the intersection of flight hardware and ground systems: the same brackets, the same drawing discipline, and the same release standard applied to both. A bracket that did not fit the real hardware was not a drafting error — it was a build stop.

Challenge

  • Tolerance acquisition was the binding constraint. Tolerance decisions required input from multiple departments, and getting a response typically took days — sometimes weeks — because the people needed were booked on their own schedules. The drawing packages had to be sequenced around those waits without slipping.
  • Scheduling compounded it. Cross-department collaboration was the only way to get a tolerance confirmed, and every confirmation depended on someone else’s availability.
  • Flight hardware and ground control stations both needed drawing packages that were right the first time — rework on this hardware is costly, and a wrong bracket discovered at assembly is the most expensive kind of wrong.

What I did

  • Designed multiple retrofitting brackets for UAV interior hardware and ground control systems. Each bracket started from the real thing: working with Senior Mechanical Engineers, Senior Manufacturing Engineers, and the vendors supplying the hardware, I gathered the brackets’ actual dimensions and developed the components to fit them.
  • Applied GD&T across most of the design and drafting work — the tolerance framework that made the brackets fit the first time, and the same stack (Creo + Windchill + GD&T) the work still runs on today.
  • Built Creo solid models and detailed 2D drawings for form, fit, and function across the UAV platforms and the ground control systems.
  • Caught pre-production flaws before build, preventing costly rework.
  • Executed engineering change orders (ECOs) and maintained drawing records in Windchill, keeping the released packages traceable from model to shop floor.
  • The brackets were approved by Mechanical Engineering for manufacturing and used in system assembly — the design loop closed in production, not just on paper.

Results

  • 750+ complex mechanical assembly part drawings released during the contract — the drawings, ECOs, and components behind them.
  • Retrofitting brackets approved by Mechanical Engineering for manufacturing and installed in system assembly across UAV hardware and ground control systems.
  • Pre-production flaws caught before build — rework avoided on flight hardware and ground stations alike.
  • Interior components delivered across multiple UAV platforms plus ground control systems, in initial-release production.

Why it matters

This is the defense-aerospace pedigree behind his current applications: the same Creo + Windchill + GD&T stack those postings ask for, and proof he has already shipped flight hardware — not just studied it.

The bracket work is the clearest version of that proof. It was not a solo drafting exercise: it took the dimensions from the vendors, the sign-off from Senior Mechanical Engineering, the manufacturing read from Senior Manufacturing Engineering, and the tolerance discipline of GD&T — and it ended with hardware in the system, not a drawing in a queue. That is the same cross-functional loop the next role asks for, already run at production scale: 750+ drawings released, in a year, against the hardest version of the constraint — waiting on other people’s schedules and still shipping.
